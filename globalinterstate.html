Core Definitions (Verbatim Slides & Audio)

Three Core Principles of the Interstate System

1. Sovereignty

Slide 9 Verbatim Meaning "In political theory, the ultimate overseer, or authority, in the decision-making process of the state and in the maintenance of order... was originally meant to be the equivalent of supreme power."[cite: 3]

Etymology: Derived from the Latin term superanus through the French term souveraineté[cite: 3]. It signifies supreme authority within domestic territory, free from external control[cite: 3].

2. Territoriality

Slide 10 Verbatim Meaning "Is a term associated with non verbal communication that refers to how people use space (territory) to communicate ownership or occupancy of areas and possessions."[cite: 3]

Philippine Context: The lecturer notes territorial disputes in the West Philippine Sea (Spratly Islands) and claims over Sabah as modern territoriality controversies[cite: 3].

3. Non-Interference / Non-Intervention

Slide 11 & Audio Verbatim "Interference is the act of or something that obstructs or hinders. Under the principle of non-interference, states in the world cannot intervene or interfere in purely domestic internal affairs of another state."[cite: 3]

Part 2: The Peace of Westphalia (1648) & Rise of the Nation-State

Historical Context & Treaties

Westphalia is a region in northwestern Germany[cite: 3]. The 1648 Peace of Westphalia ended the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648) in the Holy Roman Empire, and the Eighty Years' War (1568–1648) between Spain and the Dutch Republic, formally recognizing the independence of the Dutch Republic[cite: 3].

Three Treaties Signed:

  • The Peace of Münster[cite: 3]
  • The Treaty of Münster[cite: 3]
  • The Treaty of Osnabrück[cite: 3]

Pre-Westphalia vs. Post-Westphalia Locus of Power

  • Pre-Westphalia: Europe consisted of feudal entities; states were not the main actors[cite: 3]. At the macro level, ultimate authority resided in the Roman Catholic Church (Pope) and the Emperor of the Holy Roman Empire[cite: 3]. At the micro level, authority centered on local political units smaller than states[cite: 3].
  • Post-Westphalia: Ended the hegemony of the Habsburg Empire; the sovereign nation-state was recognized as the primary unit of international relations, recognizing no superior authority over them[cite: 3].

How the State Works (Capital and Coercion)

Slide 17: Charles Tilly "The state makes wars, and war makes the state."[cite: 3]

The state arose from the convergence of political and economic factors—namely capital and coercion (wealth and means to launch wars)[cite: 3]:

Money in Economy → Capitalist Class Money → Monarchs → Bureaucracy → Tax Collection / Standing Army → Security to Capitalists[cite: 3]

Five Principles in Politics Established by Westphalia

  1. The principle of state sovereignty[cite: 3].
  2. The principle of legal equality of states ("no sovereign among equals")[cite: 3].
  3. The principle of non-intervention of one state in the internal affairs of another[cite: 3].
  4. International law and diplomacy[cite: 3].
  5. Reason of state (raison d'état) replaced religion (separation of state and church in governance)[cite: 3].

Adoption in the United Nations Charter

  • Article 2(1): "The organization is based on the principle of the sovereign equality of all its members."[cite: 3]
  • Article 2(7): "Nothing contained in the present charter shall authorize the United Nations to intervene in matters which are essentially within the domestic jurisdiction of any state."[cite: 3]
  • Decolonization & National Consciousness: A huge wave of Asian and African states gained equal sovereign status through post-WWII decolonization (e.g., the Philippines granted independence by the US on July 4, 1946)[cite: 3].

Part 3: Challenges of Globalization to the Interstate System

Definition of Globalization (Slide 31)

Slide 31 Verbatim Definition "Broadly defined as the migration of human activities from the narrow confines of nation-state to a much larger scale... integration of national economies through trade, investment, capital flows, labor migration and technology."[cite: 4]

Two Competing Perspectives on State vs. Globalization

Outside-In View

Slide 74 Verbatim "Regards globalization as an exogenous process that is making a profound impact on states affairs. States become powerless to the forces of the market... globalization is undermining the contemporary interstate system."[cite: 4]

Inside-Out View

Slide 75 Verbatim "Recognizes that states are the ones influencing the level of global interconnectedness today... a transformative process that redefines conceptions of state, sovereignty, and territory, and does not necessarily replace them."[cite: 4]

Six Key Westphalian Challenges Under Globalization (Slide 33)

  1. Legal sovereignty is no longer the monopoly of national governments: Authority shifted upwards to supranational institutions (EU, WTO) whose rules override domestic laws[cite: 4].
  2. Control of physical territory is much less meaningful: Global telecommunications, the Internet, and digital capital flows bypass national borders[cite: 4].
  3. Non-state actors as 'new stars' of the global order: MNCs, IGOs, NGOs, as well as illicit outlaw groups (drug cartels, ethnic mafias) dictate terms to states[cite: 4].
  4. International law challenges the supremacy of state sovereignty: Human rights norms, environmental protection, and international tribunals (e.g., International Criminal Court / ICC investigating human rights violations like the Philippine drug war under Duterte) hold sovereign states accountable[cite: 3, 4].
  5. Westphalian wars are in decline, non-Westphalian conflicts on the rise: Shift from formal inter-state war to asymmetric, economic, informational, and cartel conflicts[cite: 4].
  6. Modernization: Industrialization, market infrastructure development, and political transformation driven by dominant powers (e.g., US presence in Afghanistan and the Philippines)[cite: 4].

Part 4: Evolution & Theoretical Perspectives of the Interstate System

The Cold War Era (1947–1991)

A global ideological, political, and military struggle between the two postwar superpowers: the United States (Capitalism & Democracy) and the Soviet Union / USSR (Socialism & Communism)[cite: 5].

Timeline of Crises & Proxy Conflicts Discussed:

  • 1945: Berlin Occupation and division of Germany[cite: 5].
  • 1947: Greek Civil War (US and USSR backing opposing sides)[cite: 5].
  • 1948: US President Harry S. Truman vs. Soviet Premier Joseph Stalin over rearming Germany[cite: 5].
  • 1950: People's Republic of China (PRC, Communist) vs. Republic of China (ROC, Taiwan)[cite: 5].
  • 1951: Korean War (US/South Korea vs. USSR/China/North Korea)[cite: 5].
  • 1956: Hungarian Revolution against Soviet domination[cite: 5].
  • 1959: Cuban Revolution led by Fidel Castro (M-26-7)[cite: 5].
  • 1961: Space/Rocketry Race (Titan II vs. Vostok/Soyuz) and the erection of the Berlin Wall[cite: 5].
  • 1980s: Soviet War in Afghanistan[cite: 5].
